### Research Rigor and Practical Problem Solving

The rubric applies both to projects that investigate a research question and to applications that solve a concrete problem. A strong project explains **what problem matters, why the chosen approach is appropriate, and what evidence shows that it works**. An application is not expected to invent a new model merely to satisfy research-oriented wording; thoughtful adaptation, integration, and evaluation can demonstrate a meaningful contribution.

For a research-oriented project, evidence may include controlled experiments, comparisons with published methods, and analysis of a hypothesis. For an application-oriented project, evidence may include realistic user tasks, comparison with an existing workflow, reliable end-to-end behavior, and measured improvements in quality, time, or cost. A polished interface alone is not a substitute for technical substance or evidence of usefulness.

**Read the examples as guidance, not an extra checklist.** The explanations below illustrate how to demonstrate each existing criterion; they do not introduce additional grading components. Choose evidence appropriate to your project's goals and explain when a particular technique does not apply—for example, model training when using a pretrained API, or a formal user study when no participants are available. Avoid claiming benefits that your experiments or demonstrations do not establish.

## Project Proposal (10%)

**Teams and idea paragraph due:** October 16, 2026.
**Final proposal due:** October 26, 2026 • **Maximum length:** one page.

Submit through Gradescope as a group; incorporate TA feedback into the final proposal. Credit is **completion-based**: on-time submissions and TA discussion attendance. The criteria below are **guidance, not separately scored**.

### Clarity of Objectives

- **Clear definition of research questions or development goals.** State what you want to learn or enable a user to do, and what success looks like. Prefer a focused question or task over "build an AI assistant."
- **Specific problems to be addressed.** Describe the limitation or pain point, who encounters it, and why existing methods, tools, or manual workflows do not adequately solve it.
- **Expected outcomes and deliverables.** Identify expected artifacts and evidence, such as an application, experimental results, or an evaluation dataset. Distinguish the minimum achievable outcome from optional extensions.
- **Alignment with course objectives.** Explain which generative AI concepts or system-design techniques you will apply and learn about. Connect those choices to the problem rather than simply listing technologies.

### Feasibility

- **Realistic timeline and milestones.** Plan achievable stages, such as data preparation, a baseline, a prototype, and evaluation. Leave time to diagnose failures and improve the system.
- **Assessment of required resources and their availability.** Identify required data, compute, APIs, software, and access permissions, and explain how the plan fits your team's available resources and budget.
- **Identification of potential challenges and mitigation strategies.** Identify likely obstacles, such as limited data, unreliable outputs, or integration difficulties. Suggest a fallback or smaller experiment that would still yield useful results.
- **Clear scope definition.** Specify which tasks, users, and conditions the project covers and excludes. Prefer a well-evaluated solution to a focused problem over many unfinished features.

### Innovation and Relevance

- **Originality of the proposed approach.** Explain your contribution beyond copying an existing tutorial or application. This may be a research idea, meaningful adaptation, or thoughtful integration for a specific need.
- **Connection to current GenAI research trends.** Relate the approach to relevant recent methods or system practices and explain why they fit. Using the newest model is not itself a design justification.
- **Potential impact on the field.** Describe who benefits and what improves, whether scientific understanding or a particular user workflow. Keep claims proportional to the project's intended scope.
- **Novel application or methodology.** Identify the distinctive task, setting, or methodological choice, and explain why it raises a worthwhile question or addresses a gap in off-the-shelf solutions.

### 1. Problem Statement (10%)

#### Context and Background

- **Clear description of the problem domain.** Introduce the setting and enough background for a classmate unfamiliar with the area to understand the task. For an application, use a concrete example of the workflow or user problem.
- **Current state of the art and its limitations.** Describe relevant research methods, existing products, or the current manual process, and identify what they do poorly. Compare against realistic alternatives rather than assuming that no solution exists.
- **Significance of the problem in GenAI context.** Explain why generative AI is a suitable part of the solution and what difficulty or opportunity it introduces. Make clear where it adds value relative to a simpler non-generative approach.
- **Target audience and use cases.** Identify the intended users and the situations in which they would use the result. Describe representative tasks and constraints, such as response time, domain knowledge, accessibility, or data sensitivity.

#### Research Questions

- **Clearly articulated research questions.** Frame a question that your evaluation can answer, whether it concerns a model property or an application's effectiveness. For example, ask whether retrieval improves answer grounding or whether a workflow reduces task-completion time.
- **Hypotheses and assumptions.** State what you expect to happen and the conditions on which that expectation depends. Separate assumptions about data, users, or model behavior from facts that you have actually tested.
- **Scope and boundaries of the investigation.** Explain which cases your conclusions cover and which remain outside the study. A prototype tested on a small dataset should not be presented as proven reliable for all users or deployment settings.

### 2. Major Contributions (10%)

#### Technical Innovations

- **Novel algorithms or methodologies.** Explain the technical idea or problem-solving method your team developed or adapted and why it is useful. An application may contribute a task-specific pipeline or evaluation method without proposing a new learning algorithm.
- **Architectural improvements.** Show how the organization of models, retrieval, tools, state, or interface components supports the task. Explain the reason for important design choices rather than merely displaying a system diagram.
- **Performance enhancements.** Identify improvements over an earlier version or baseline, using relevant measures such as answer quality, latency, or task success. Explain which changes produced the improvement and what trade-offs they introduced.
- **Integration strategies.** Describe how the components work together and how information passes between them. For practical applications, explain how you handled issues such as incompatible data formats, tool failures, or maintaining context across steps.

#### Practical Impact

- **Real-world applications.** Demonstrate how the work addresses a realistic task rather than only a convenient toy example. If deployment or actual user access is outside scope, explain what the prototype establishes and what remains untested.
- **Scalability considerations.** Discuss what would change with more users, larger datasets, or longer inputs, and identify likely bottlenecks. Small-scale measurements or a reasoned analysis are more credible than unsupported claims of production readiness.
- **Resource efficiency.** Explain the compute, memory, API, or operating costs relevant to your approach. Discuss whether the quality gained justifies those costs and whether a simpler configuration could meet the same need.
- **User experience improvements.** Show how the application makes a task easier, clearer, or less error-prone for its intended users. Useful improvements may include fewer manual steps, understandable outputs, source visibility, or ways to recover from mistakes.

### 3. Evaluation (10%)

#### Quantitative Metrics

- **Performance measurements.** Choose metrics that reflect the project goal, such as accuracy, groundedness, task completion, response time, or cost per task. Explain how each metric was measured and why it captures something important to the research question or user.
- **Comparative analysis with baselines.** Compare your approach with a meaningful alternative under comparable conditions, such as a simple prompt, an existing model, or the current workflow. Explain both improvements and situations where the baseline remains preferable.
- **Statistical significance of results.** Show whether observed differences are consistent rather than likely artifacts of a few favorable examples. Where appropriate, use repeated trials, variability estimates, confidence intervals, or statistical tests; for small practical evaluations, report the sample size and uncertainty without overstating conclusions.
- **Ablation studies.** Remove or vary important components to learn which choices actually help, such as retrieval, reranking, prompt design, or a validation step. A focused comparison of application configurations can serve this purpose without retraining a model.

#### Qualitative Analysis

- **Case studies.** Walk through representative examples to explain how and why the system succeeds or fails. Include challenging cases as well as successful ones so the audience can judge more than the best-case demonstration.
- **User studies, if applicable.** If you evaluate with users, describe who participated, what tasks they performed, and how feedback was collected. If no user study was conducted, distinguish your team's scenario-based testing from evidence about actual user satisfaction.
- **Error analysis.** Group recurring failures and investigate their likely causes, such as missing context, incorrect retrieval, or brittle tool use. Explain what those failures reveal about the method and what you changed or would change to address them.
- **Limitations and future work.** State the conditions in which the system or conclusions are unreliable, incomplete, or untested. Prioritize follow-up work based on observed limitations rather than presenting an unrelated list of possible features.

## Project Demo (30%)

**Demo schedule:** use the [Presentation and Demo Sign-Up Sheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1z0Hv-ZGGtXJgJVtnYwnykvpl9K9pEFie8C-6XHNOO1A/edit?usp=sharing) for your **shared presentation/demo slot**. The demo is assessed separately from the final presentation and evaluates **demonstrated working functionality, with live execution strongly preferred**. Code quality is assessed through the [GitHub submission](#github-repository-15), **not during the presentation or demo slot**.

**Keep it short and focused.** A brief end-to-end demonstration of the core use case is sufficient. Run the actual system with representative input and show the result, not only slides, screenshots, or a UI mockup; include the runnable demo code in your GitHub repository.

**Rehearse for a reliable live demo.** The UI (if applicable) and underlying workflow should run without crashes or hangs. Check model/API access, dependencies, data, and connectivity in advance. Demonstrate usable response times and support any latency or reliability claims with measurements; handle expected failures gracefully rather than letting them crash the session.

### Explanation and Demonstration (15%)

- **Clear explanation of the functionality.** Introduce the intended user, the task, and the expected result. Explain the key steps as you demonstrate them so the audience can follow what the tool does and why it is useful.
- **Working end-to-end functionality.** Run representative input through the actual system and show the output. Make clear which proposed features work; distinguish live execution, backup recordings, mockups, hard-coded outputs, and planned extensions.
- **Smooth and focused demonstration.** Use a prepared, coherent sequence without unnecessary detours or lengthy setup during the slot. A short demonstration that clearly shows the core use case is sufficient.
- **Backup recording allowed.** You may prepare a recording of the actual working system as a backup if technical issues interrupt the live demo; clearly identify recorded material. A **successful live demo earns higher credit** than a recording-only demonstration of comparable quality.

### Simplicity, Reliability, and Usability (15%)

- **Concise, purposeful feature design.** Keep the tool focused on its core task. Avoid unnecessary features, screens, configuration, or interaction steps; more features do not automatically earn a higher score.
- **Reliable live behavior.** The demonstrated UI or CLI and underlying workflow should run without crashes, hangs, or broken transitions. Handle expected failures with clear messages and safe recovery; support reliability claims with evidence beyond one successful run.
- **Easy to start and use.** Show a straightforward path from launching the tool to completing a useful task. Inputs, controls, and outputs should be understandable, with minimal user effort and no dependence on unexplained presenter-only workarounds.
- **Responsive user experience.** Keep waiting times reasonable for the task and make progress or loading states clear. Any latency claims should match measured behavior, not just a prepared best-case example.

## GitHub Repository (15%)

**Due:** December 15, 2026. Include the **actual runnable demo/application code**, not just experiments: the repository must also contain evaluation code, results, and documentation needed to reproduce the work and run the demonstrated system.

### Demo/Application Code Quality (5%)

- **Organization and readability.** Write reviewable, modular code that separates core logic, UI, and evaluation, avoiding needless complexity and repeated work.
- **Documentation and maintainability.** Document interfaces and non-obvious choices so others can understand and extend the demonstrated implementation.
- **Tests and error handling.** Include unit and integration tests, demo smoke tests, and clear handling of invalid inputs or service failures.

### Experiment Results and Data (5%)

- **Data and reproducibility.** Include shareable data or access/preprocessing instructions; record configurations, model versions, prompts, and reproduction limits.
- **Results and analysis.** Provide baseline comparisons, metrics, representative outputs, and failure cases, with runnable scripts or notebooks producing the analysis.

### Step-by-Step Tutorial (5%)

- **Setup and launch.** Give tested installation and demo-launch commands, prerequisites, hardware/API costs, and placeholder settings; never commit secrets.
- **Use and troubleshooting.** Include sample inputs, UI/CLI steps, expected outputs, experiment commands, known limitations, and recovery instructions.

### Suggested Repository Organization

Example only; adapt the names and language to your project. The demo should use the shared implementation in `src/`.

```
my-genai-project/
|-- README.md                 # Setup, demo and experiment commands

|   |-- sample_inputs.json    # Representative demo inputs
```

## Final Report (15%)

**Due:** December 15, 2026. The report should explain the research or practical problem-solving process, methodology, results, and implications. The five categories below total 15% of the final project grade.

### Paper Format and Templates

For an academic-paper layout, we recommend the [ACM Master Article Template](https://www.acm.org/publications/proceedings-template) or [IEEE Conference Paper Templates](https://www.ieee.org/conferences/publishing/templates) (LaTeX or Microsoft Word). Keep the selected template's default layout, fonts, and citation style, and replace its sample text with your project's content.

### 1. Structure and Format (1%)

- **Professional layout and formatting.** Use a readable, consistent layout that makes the document easy to navigate. Presentation quality should support understanding, not substitute for technical substance.
- **Logical flow of sections.** Connect the problem, background, approach, evaluation, and conclusions in a logical order. For applications, trace the user's need through system design to evidence of effectiveness.
- **Proper citations and references.** Credit relevant papers, models, datasets, libraries, and external implementations. Distinguish borrowed ideas and components from your team's contributions.
- **Clear figures and tables.** Provide readable labels, captions, units, and context. Use figures and tables to explain architecture, summarize results, or reveal patterns rather than merely decorate the report.

### 2. Depth of Research (4%)

- **Comprehensive literature review.** Cover relevant prior work and practical alternatives rather than maximizing the reference count. Useful sources may include research papers, technical documentation, and existing systems.
- **Critical analysis of related work.** Compare the strengths, weaknesses, and assumptions of relevant approaches, and explain how this comparison informed your choices rather than merely summarizing sources.
- **Understanding of fundamental concepts.** Explain the ideas behind your techniques and why they suit the task. Using a framework or API should not replace understanding its behavior and limitations.
- **Integration of multiple perspectives.** Consider relevant perspectives such as model quality, reliability, cost, and user needs. Explain how competing considerations shaped the final approach.

### Alignment and Feedback

Keep the report aligned with the October 26 final proposal, revised after your TA discussion. TAs will record feedback from your proposal discussion; the final evaluation will consider whether important feedback was addressed. Show how that feedback and subsequent project work informed the final presentation, demo, and report.

### 3. Methodology (5%)

- **Detailed experimental setup.** Describe configurations, data splits or test cases, baselines, and evaluation procedures. For applications, include the end-to-end workflow and the conditions under which it was tested.
- **Data collection and preprocessing.** Explain data sources, selection, cleaning, and coverage limitations. Where relevant, discuss permissions, sensitive information, and separation of evaluation data from development decisions.
- **Model architecture and implementation.** Describe the models, component interactions, and key implementation choices. For pretrained models or APIs, document relevant versions, prompts, retrieval settings, and tool interfaces.
- **Training and optimization procedures.** For training or fine-tuning, describe the objective, configuration, and model selection. Otherwise, explain how you iterated on prompts, retrieval, orchestration, or other settings and evaluated those choices.

### 4. Results and Analysis (4%)

- **Clear presentation of findings.** Report the main outcomes and connect them to the original goals or questions. Distinguish measured results from observations, expectations, and features that were implemented but not evaluated.
- **Appropriate visualizations.** Use plots, tables, or annotated examples that make the relevant comparisons and trade-offs visible. Choose a format that helps readers interpret the evidence, including failures or variability where relevant.
- **Thorough analysis of results.** Explain why the results may have occurred, how they compare with baselines, and where the approach falls short. For applications, analyze whether a measured improvement translates into a meaningful benefit for the intended task.
- **Discussion of implications.** Explain what the findings suggest for future research, system design, or practical use. Keep recommendations within the limits of the evaluation and identify what additional evidence would be needed for broader claims.

### 5. Grammar and Writing Quality (1%)

- **Professional academic tone.** Use precise, evidence-based language appropriate for a technical report, including application-focused work. Avoid promotional claims such as "revolutionary" or "production-ready" unless the evidence supports the underlying claim.
- **Clear and concise writing.** Make each paragraph advance the explanation, and remove repetition or unnecessary detail. A reader should be able to identify the problem, approach, contribution, and result without searching through vague descriptions.
- **Proper technical terminology.** Use terms accurately and define abbreviations or domain-specific concepts when first introduced. Distinguish, for example, training from inference and a model's output quality from the success of the overall application.
- **Consistent formatting.** Keep notation, terminology, headings, citations, and figure references consistent throughout the report. A final editing pass should make contributions from different team members read as one coherent document.
